r₀ is the spacing where the atoms sit when nothing pushes or pulls on them. Bond energy is the energy needed to break one mole (6 × 10²³) of these bonds, in kJ/mol. A deeper energy well means a stronger bond. The steeper the sides of the well, the stiffer the material.

Try this in class
  1. Compare W and Hg. Both are metals. Why does one melt at 3414 °C and the other is liquid at room temperature? W's well is about 14 times deeper (850 vs 62 kJ/mol). It takes much more heat to shake the atoms out of a deep well.
  2. Inside a Cl₂ molecule the bond is 121 kJ/mol, but Cl₂ melts at −101 °C. Why so low? Melting only has to break the weak van der Waals bonds between molecules (31 kJ/mol). The strong bond inside each molecule stays intact.
  3. Pull the atoms past the top of the force curve. What happens to the force you need? It drops. Past the peak the bond gives way, so the peak force is the bond's strength.

Try this in class
  1. Choose “Only what's inside the cell” for FCC and click a corner atom, then a face atom. How many whole atoms fit in one cell? 8 corners × ⅛ + 6 faces × ½ = 4 atoms.
  2. Choose “One atom's neighbours” for FCC, BCC and HCP. Count the neighbours. FCC 12, BCC 8, HCP 12. More touching neighbours means tighter packing.
  3. Pick copper. Compare the density computed from the cell with the measured value. 8.93 vs 8.94 g/cm³. A unit cell a third of a nanometre across predicts the density of a block you can hold.
  4. Heat iron past 912 °C. Which structure packs more tightly? FCC (74% of space filled vs 68% for BCC), so iron shrinks slightly when it switches.

Stack close-packed layers

Every close-packed layer looks the same: each atom touches six others. A new layer settles into the dips of the one below. There are two sets of dips to choose from, and that choice decides the crystal. Layer positions are called A, B and C. The coloured dots on the top layer show where the next layer can sit.

Try this in class
  1. Build A, B, then put the third layer back over A. Keep going. What do you get? ABAB… is hexagonal close-packed (HCP): Mg, Ti, Zn.
  2. Now put the third layer in the C dips instead. What do you get? ABCABC… is face-centred cubic (FCC): Al, Cu, Ni. The FCC cube's densest plane is exactly this layer.
  3. Look at the stacking-fault example from the side. Can you see where it went wrong? ABCAB|ABC: one layer sits where HCP would put it. That mistake in the stacking is a planar defect called a stacking fault.

Try this in class
  1. Click a few atoms to remove them. What colour ring appears around each hole? Amber: each neighbour of a vacancy has lost one of its 6 neighbours.
  2. Melt, then cool slowly. Melt again, then quench. Switch the colour to grain orientation. Which has more, smaller grains? The quench. Many crystals start growing at once and meet, so the grains are small. Slow cooling gives fewer, larger grains.
  3. Switch to two sizes, melt, then quench. Does it crystallise? Mostly not. The mixed sizes cannot find a repeating pattern before they freeze, so you get a glass (an amorphous solid).
  4. Find an amber atom touching a teal one inside a grain. What is it? A 5–7 pair marks a dislocation in this kind of layer: the end of an extra row of atoms.

Why metals bend at a fraction of their ideal strength

Sliding the top half of a crystal over the bottom half all at once means breaking every bond across the slip plane at the same time. A dislocation, the edge of an extra half-plane of atoms (red), lets the slip happen one bond at a time, like pushing a wrinkle across a rug.

Stress is on a schematic 0–100 scale, where 100 is the ideal strength of a perfect crystal. Real values depend on the metal. The Burgers circuit takes the same number of atom steps right, up, left and down; if it does not close, the gap is the Burgers vector b.

Try this in class
  1. With a dislocation, how much stress does it take to slip the crystal? And for the whole plane at once? A small push moves the dislocation. The whole plane needs nearly the ideal strength, because every bond across the plane breaks at the same moment.
  2. Turn on the Burgers circuit and step the dislocation into it. What happens to the circuit? It no longer closes. The missing step is the Burgers vector, one atom spacing along the slip direction.
  3. When the dislocation leaves the crystal, look at the right-hand edge. What is left behind? A step one atom spacing high. Millions of these steps make the slip lines you can see on a deformed metal.
  4. Add the solute atom, then the grain boundary. What does each one do? Both pin the dislocation, so a higher stress is needed to keep it moving. That is solid-solution and grain-size strengthening.
