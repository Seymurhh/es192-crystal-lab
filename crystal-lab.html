r = 1.00 r₀

r₀ is the spacing where the atoms sit when nothing pushes or pulls on them. Bond energy (Callister's “bonding energy”, Table 2.3) is the energy needed to pull one mole (6 × 10²³) of the solid's atoms, ions or molecules completely apart, in kJ/mol. A deeper energy well means a stronger bond. The steeper the force curve where it crosses zero at r₀, the stiffer the material (Callister Eq. 6.6).

About this model

Bond energies and melting points: Callister & Rethwisch, Materials Science and Engineering, 10th ed., Table 2.3. Ar and Kr were measured below atmospheric pressure.

Bond energies are per mole of atoms, formula units or molecules, not per single bond: CaF₂'s 1548 kJ/mol covers two Ca–F bonds, and water's 51 kJ/mol about two hydrogen bonds per molecule.

The curve shapes are a teaching schematic (one Morse-type shape scaled by each bond energy). They show trends, not measured curves.

A live raft of atoms

A flat layer of about 1,800 atoms that attract and repel like real ones (a Lennard-Jones model: atoms pull on each other when slightly apart and push hard when squeezed together; soap bubbles floating on water behave in a similar way, so a bubble raft forms the same crystals and defects). Heat it and it melts. Cool it and it crystallises. How you cool it decides what you get.

About this model

Temperatures are in reduced units: at 1.0, each atom's typical jiggling energy equals the energy of one bond. This raft melts at about 1. The simulation runs about 1,800 atoms in a box that wraps around at the edges, so there are no free surfaces. “In crystal sites” counts atoms with exactly six neighbours arranged as a regular hexagon. The box cannot shrink, and the crystal packs a few per cent denser than the liquid, so freezing leaves small holes, mostly along grain boundaries, like shrinkage pores in a casting.

Why metals bend at a fraction of their ideal strength

Sliding the top half of a crystal over the bottom half all at once means breaking every bond across the slip plane at the same time. A dislocation, the edge of an extra half-plane of atoms (red), lets the slip happen one bond at a time, like pushing a wrinkle across a rug.

Obstacles and tools

Stress is on a schematic 0–100 scale; about 95 is the ideal strength, the stress needed to slide a whole plane of a perfect crystal at once. The real gap is far larger than 8 versus 95: a pure copper crystal starts to slip at about 1 MPa, thousands of times below its ideal shear strength of several GPa. The Burgers circuit takes as many atom steps left as right, and as many down as up; if it does not close, the gap is the Burgers vector b. The shaded region is a second grain; in a real metal its rows of atoms are tilted, but they are drawn straight here for simplicity.

Show

Drawn on a simple cubic lattice, as in Callister's Figs 4.4–4.6, so the planes are easy to follow; the bottom half is held still and each atom of the top half is coloured by how far it has moved: grey not yet, blue by one full b, in between at the dislocation. The shear stress (black arrows) pushes the top half toward +x. The dislocation line is the boundary between the part of the slip plane that has already slipped and the part that has not. Edge: b at right angles to the line, so the line moves along b. Screw: b parallel to the line, so the line moves across b. Mixed: one curved line that is edge at one face and screw at the other, with the same b everywhere. In copper (FCC), b = (a/2)⟨110⟩, 0.256 nm long, and slip is on {111} planes.

Show

Annealed copper (E = 110 GPa, G = 46 GPa, b = 0.256 nm). Each grain has a random crystal orientation. Pulling along the bar resolves a shear stress τ = σ cos φ cos λ (Schmid's law, Callister Eq. 7.2) on each of its 12 {111}⟨110⟩ slip systems; a grain slips when τ on its best system reaches the stress its dislocations need, τ = τ₀ + αGb√ρ (α = 0.3, plus a Hall–Petch term k/√d with k = 0.11 MPa·m½). Slipping multiplies dislocations (density ρ rises from about 10¹² toward 10¹⁵ m⁻²), which is strain hardening. Every grain feels the same stress (the Sachs approximation; real neighbouring grains also push on each other). Necking starts when the curve's slope falls to the stress itself (Considère's criterion). Model curve: yield ≈ 68 MPa and tensile strength ≈ 206 MPa, close to Callister Table 6.1 (69 and 200 MPa).

    A phase diagram is a map. Pick a composition (across) and a temperature (up), and it tells you which phases are stable, what each one is made of, and how much of each there is. A phase is a region of uniform structure and composition: a liquid, or one kind of crystal.

    About this diagram

    The micrographs are simulations of slow (equilibrium) cooling, not photographs. The dendrites come from a phase-field model of solidification (Kobayashi, 1993), the eutectic and pearlite plates from a stripe-forming model seeded as colonies, and the steel grains, twins, ferrite and cementite from grain-boundary growth maps. Each structure is drawn at the lever-rule amount at that point, converted from mass to area (the lead-rich and tin-rich phases differ in density by a third). Grey levels follow the usual etched appearance: lead-rich α dark and tin-rich β bright; ferrite and cementite white and pearlite dark. “Real photo” shows a published micrograph of the closest real alloy.
